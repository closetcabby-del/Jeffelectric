Is Charging an EV Really Cheaper Than Buying Gas?

We ran a simple Texas comparison using public energy data so homeowners can see where the savings actually come from—and where they can disappear.

Updated August 2026 · Approx. 5 minute read

You’ve probably heard it before: “Electric cars are cheaper to fuel.” But cheaper by how much? And once that EV is plugged into your house, are you really saving money—or just replacing the gas station with a bigger electric bill?

The short answer

For a driver who primarily charges at home, an EV can cost substantially less per mile to fuel than a typical gasoline vehicle.

Let’s compare 100 miles.

For this example, we’ll use an EV that consumes 30 kWh per 100 miles, a Texas residential electricity rate of 16.44¢ per kWh, a gasoline vehicle getting 30 MPG, and Houston regular gasoline at $3.462 per gallon.

EV charged at home$4.93Estimated energy cost per 100 miles
30-MPG gas vehicle$11.54Estimated fuel cost per 100 miles
That’s about 57% less for the EV in this example.

It is an illustration, not a universal savings guarantee. Your vehicle, electricity plan, gas price and charging habits all matter.

Where the EV number comes from

The U.S. Department of Energy explains EV efficiency in kilowatt-hours used per 100 miles. A reasonable current range for light-duty EVs is roughly 25–40 kWh per 100 miles, depending on the vehicle and driving conditions.

30 kWh × $0.1644 = $4.93 per 100 miles

Using the Texas residential electricity average in this example, 30 kWh of driving energy costs about $4.93.

Now compare the gas station

A 30-MPG gasoline vehicle needs about 3.33 gallons to travel 100 miles.

100 miles ÷ 30 MPG = 3.33 gallons
3.33 × $3.462 = about $11.54

What happens over a year?

Small differences per trip become meaningful when you drive thousands of miles.

12,000 miles/yearEstimated annual cost
EV at home$592
30-MPG gas vehicle$1,385
Estimated difference≈ $793/year

At 15,000 miles per year, the gap in this same simplified example grows to roughly $990 per year.

But won’t an EV make my electric bill explode?

Your electric bill will go up because you’re buying driving energy from your utility instead of a gas station. The better question is whether that increase is larger than what you used to spend on gasoline.

In this example, the EV adds about $4.93 in electricity for every 100 miles, while the gasoline vehicle burns about $11.54 in fuel over the same distance.

The charger itself isn’t “using all the electricity.”

A Level 2 charger is better thought of as the path that safely delivers power to the car. The vehicle determines how much energy it needs.

Not all EVs cost the same to charge.

Just like a compact sedan and a pickup do not use the same amount of gasoline.

Here’s where the answer changes: public fast charging.

Home charging and public DC fast charging are not the same financial proposition. Public charging prices can be materially higher because the operator is also paying for commercial equipment, infrastructure, property, networking, maintenance and business operations.

That means someone who charges mostly at home can have a very different cost experience from someone who relies heavily on public fast chargers.

What if the gas car gets 40 MPG?

Now the comparison gets closer. At $3.462 per gallon, a 40-MPG gasoline vehicle costs about $8.66 per 100 miles. Our EV example remains about $4.93.

Compare that with a 25-MPG gasoline vehicle, which works out to roughly $13.85 per 100 miles. The vehicle you are replacing matters almost as much as the EV you are buying.

Do you automatically need a new panel for an EV charger?

No. Buying an EV does not automatically mean your electrical panel needs to be replaced. The right answer depends on your charger, existing electrical loads, available breaker space, service capacity and wiring route.

The 30-second takeaway

Using the assumptions in this article, home EV charging comes out to about $4.93 per 100 miles versus about $11.54 for a 30-MPG gasoline vehicle—roughly a 57% lower energy cost in this specific comparison.

The real answer for your driveway depends on four things: your EV, your electricity plan, how much you drive and where you charge.
